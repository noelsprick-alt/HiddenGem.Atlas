My story in Saigon (Ho Chi Minh City)

Saigon was the opposite of Hoi An: the city never stops, and that is exactly the appeal. My advice there: make no plan, drift through District 1 and stroll Nguyễn Huệ boulevard at night.

Getting there and around

Saigon (Ho Chi Minh City) has a large airport, Tan Son Nhat; domestic flights from Hanoi and Da Nang are cheap.

From Tân Sơn Nhất airport you are in the centre in about 30–60 minutes, depending on traffic. The easiest options are Grab (the Vietnamese taxi app) or official taxis; Metro Line 1 connects parts of the city but is not yet usable everywhere. For Germans a visa-free entry for a limited time usually applies – check the current rules beforehand.

In the city Grab cars and Grab Bike (scooter) are the most convenient way to get around; buses exist but are hard for beginners to figure out. Pay in dong where possible and carry small notes.

1. Must-sees

District 1

Make no plan, drift.

District 1 is the centre of Ho Chi Minh City: skyscrapers next to French colonial buildings, coffee shops next to street kitchens, streams of scooters in between. Almost all sights in this guide are here, most reachable on foot or by short taxi rides.

The trick when crossing the street: walk slowly and steadily, do not run and do not stop – the scooters flow around you. Plan breaks in air-conditioned cafés; the heat and humidity are draining.

Tip: Set off in the early morning – from noon it is hot and the lanes are crowded.

Nguyễn Huệ at night🆓 Free to experience

The boulevard in the evening: families, music, light.

Nguyễn Huệ is a wide pedestrian promenade leading from the City Hall (People’s Committee) to the Saigon River. In the evening it fills with families, street performers, photographers and vendors; on holidays there are lights and events. Along the promenade are cafés and the famous building at No. 42 with its many small cafés on the upper floors.

At the river end of the promenade you see the skyline with the Bitexco Tower. A walk here is free and especially worthwhile at sunset. Watch your bags and phones – as everywhere in busy tourist areas, pickpocketing is possible.

Tip: Come at sunset and stay until dark – then the promenade glows.

Ben Thanh Market🆓 Free to experience

Ben Thanh Market is the best-known market of the city, in a hall opened in 1914 with a clock tower. Inside, stalls crowd together with clothing, fabrics, bags, coffee, spices, dried fruit and souvenirs, plus food areas with noodle soups, spring rolls and sweet dishes.

Haggling is the rule here: start at about half the quoted price and stay friendly. The market is touristy and prices are higher than at markets off the tourist track, but as an introduction it is very good. In the evening an outdoor night market with food stalls opens; keep bags and valuables in front of you.

Tip: Come in the morning when it is cooler and less crowded, and eat a noodle soup there.

War Remnants Museum

The museum documents the Vietnam War from the Vietnamese point of view: photographs, documents, military vehicles and aircraft in the yard. Especially intense are the photo exhibitions and the section on the consequences of Agent Orange. The perspective is unambiguous, but the museum is historically important.

It is not easy material – plan about two hours and a break afterwards. Admission and opening times change (usually under €1.50 per person; check beforehand). With children you should consider whether to show all rooms.

Tip: Come early in the morning or later in the afternoon and plan a quiet break afterwards.

Notre-Dame Cathedral & Central Post Office🆓 Free to experience

The red-brick cathedral was built by the French at the end of the 19th century; the bricks reportedly came from Marseille. In front stands a statue of Mary, and the twin towers are a landmark of the city. The cathedral was recently restored – whether you can go inside or whether it is still scaffolded you should check beforehand.

Directly opposite stands the Central Post Office of 1891, a French colonial building with a high hall, wall maps and wooden phone booths. It is often said that Gustave Eiffel designed it; that is disputed. You can go in, write postcards and send them – a nice moment to catch your breath.

Tip: Write a postcard at the post office – stamps are cheap, and the hall is nicer than the square outside.

Reunification Palace (Independence Palace)

Here the Vietnam War ended on 30 April 1975, when tanks of the North Vietnamese army broke through the gates. The palace from the 1960s still stands largely as it was then: conference rooms, reception halls, the president’s residence, a basement bunker with a communications room and maps.

The building is a good example of the architecture of the time, and the tanks in the garden recall the end of the war. Plan about one to two hours; audio guides are available in several languages, admission and opening times change (check beforehand).

Tip: Get an audio guide and go to the basement bunker first.

Bitexco Financial Tower & Saigon Skydeck

With its shape meant to recall a lotus bud, the Bitexco Tower is a landmark of the skyline. From the viewing platform in the Skydeck (around the 49th floor) you see Saigon from above: river, high-rises, street grid and – on clear days – far into the surrounding country.

The visit is most beautiful shortly before sunset, when the light turns warm and the lights come on. Admission is much more expensive than in the museums (roughly in the double-digit euro range; check prices beforehand). Alternatively you can visit a café or bar in a high-rise with a view – that is often cheaper.

Tip: Come 30–45 minutes before sunset and stay until dusk.

FAQ

Do I need a plan in Saigon?

Noel’s advice: no, drift.

How many days do you need in Saigon?
Two days are enough for the main sights; with a trip to the Cu Chi Tunnels or the Mekong Delta it is three.
Is Saigon safe?
Generally yes, but pickpocketing and phone snatching from scooters are possible in tourist districts. Do not hold your phone in your hand at the roadside and use bags with zips.
How do I pay in Saigon?
Cash in dong is standard in markets and small eateries; bigger restaurants and hotels often take cards. ATMs are everywhere, check your bank’s fees.
