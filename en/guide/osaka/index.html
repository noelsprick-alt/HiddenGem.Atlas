Getting there and around

You reach Osaka by shinkansen or flight; from Kyoto it is only 15–30 minutes by train. Check current timetables beforehand.

From Kansai Airport (KIX) you take the train in about 40 to 50 minutes into the city centre (Namba). Within the city you use the metro and the JR Loop Line; an IC card (for example ICOCA) makes paying easy. Osaka is very central: you reach Kyoto and Nara by train in 15 to 50 minutes (check timetables beforehand).

Check whether an Osaka pass is worthwhile for your plans – it often includes free admission to several attractions and rides. Cash is still important in Osaka; smaller stalls often take only yen.

1. Must-sees

Dotonbori

In the evening drift through the food stalls and eat takoyaki straight from the stand.

Dotonbori is the heart of Osaka’s entertainment district: a canal lined with neon signs, restaurants and food stalls. The most famous motif is the Glico Running Man sign above the water; a giant crab on the front of the Kani Doraku restaurant is the second. After dark everything glows, and the street is full.

Try takoyaki (octopus balls) and okonomiyaki (savoury pancake) right at the stall. The city’s motto is “kuidaore” – to eat until you drop. Plan an evening and let yourself drift; come around 6 p.m. when the lights come on.

Tip: Come at dusk and take small portions at several stalls instead of one big meal.

Kuromon Market

By day across the market: seafood and street food. 4.1 stars on Google; visitors say it is touristy now, early in the morning is better.

Kuromon Market is also called “Osaka’s kitchen”: a covered market hall with about 150 stalls for fish, seafood, vegetables and ready meals. You can try freshly grilled scallops, sushi, fruit on a stick and tamagoyaki.

Today the market is also very popular with tourists, so it gets crowded at midday – a visit in the early morning is better. Many stalls have limited opening hours; some close in the afternoon.

🕒 daily about 9 am–5 pm according to Google

Tip: Come before 10 a.m. and bring cash for small tasting portions.

Osaka Castle

The castle by day.

Osaka Castle was built in the 16th century by Toyotomi Hideyoshi and reconstructed in the 20th century. The main keep houses a museum about the history of the castle and the samurai era; from the top floor you have an all-round view over the city. The complex is surrounded by moats, walls and a wide park.

The gardens (for example the Nishinomaru Garden) are especially popular during the cherry blossom in spring. Plan two to three hours; admission and opening times change (check beforehand). You reach it conveniently by train.

Tip: Come in the morning when it is still cool and empty, and plan time for the park.

Shinsekai & Tsutenkaku Tower

Shinsekai is a retro district with neon signs, arcades and snack bars that recalls an earlier era. At its centre stands the Tsutenkaku Tower, a landmark of Osaka with a viewing platform. The district is known for kushikatsu – deep-fried skewers that you dip in a shared sauce. The rule there: no double dipping!

The district is very lively and authentic; in the evening it feels especially atmospheric. Plan an evening; keep an eye on your bags.

Tip: Eat kushikatsu and follow the “no double dipping” rule.

📍 View in Google Maps

Umeda Sky Building & Floating Garden

The Umeda Sky Building consists of two towers connected at the top by a platform. The Floating Garden Observatory lies about 170 metres up and offers a 360-degree view over Osaka. The view is especially beautiful at sunset and in the evening.

An escalator that runs freely through the air is an experience in itself. Admission changes (check beforehand). Plan an hour.

Tip: Come shortly before sunset and stay until the lights come on.

📍 View in Google Maps

Shinsaibashi & Amerikamura

Shinsaibashi is Osaka’s big shopping street: a covered arcade with shops, drugstores and cafés, right next to Dotonbori. Next door lies Amerikamura, a district for fashion, streetwear and youth culture with colourful shops and street art.

If you want to browse, plan the afternoon for it. Shops usually open around 10 to 11 a.m. and close in the evening. There are many opportunities to buy souvenirs.

Tip: Come in the afternoon and combine the stroll with Dotonbori in the evening.

FAQ

Is one day enough in Osaka?

For food and highlights yes.

Is one day enough in Osaka?
For the classics yes, but two to three days are better – with Nara or Kyoto as a day trip.
Do I need cash in Osaka?
Yes, small stalls and snack bars often take only yen. ATMs in convenience stores (7-Eleven, Lawson) usually work with foreign cards.
