Getting there and around

You reach Lübeck by regional train from Hamburg in about 45 minutes; you walk the old-town island.

From Hamburg you reach Lübeck by regional express in about 40 minutes; the station is about a 10-minute walk from the Holstentor. The old town is an island on which everything is easily reachable on foot (check timetables beforehand).

If you come by car, park outside the old town and walk in. A regional train runs to Travemünde.

1. Must-sees

Holstentor

The landmark from 1478.

The Holstentor was completed in 1478 and, with its two round towers, is the landmark of the city. It once stood on the edge of the city fortifications, today it is the way into the centre from the old-town island. Over the centuries the gate has sunk – you see this in the slightly crooked line of the building.

Inside is a museum showing the history of the city and the Hanseatic League (check times and prices beforehand). The best photo works from the opposite side of the water, best in the morning or in soft evening light.

Tip: Take the photo from the other side, then walk across the bridge and through the gate.

Niederegger – Marzipan

The original house with café. 4.4 stars on Google from over 8,500 reviews; guests praise cake and almond cake, it is busy in Advent.

Niederegger has existed since 1806; the main house on the market square is a café, shop and small marzipan museum in one. Try the marzipan classic, nut cake or tortes and look at the marzipan figures upstairs. Marzipan is a Lübeck landmark; many visitors take a pack home.

Especially at Christmas time and on weekends there are queues. Come early in the morning or on a weekday; ask for samples.

Tip: Come early or on a weekday, in Advent it is very crowded.

St Mary’s Church (Marienkirche)

The Marienkirche is the main church of the city and a masterpiece of North German brick Gothic. Its vault is about 38 metres high. Especially worth seeing are the astronomical clock and the fragment of the Dance of Death. It was badly damaged in the Second World War and rebuilt afterwards.

A lift leads up the tower, from which you look over the old town (check times and prices beforehand). The church became the model for many brick churches on the Baltic.

Tip: Take the lift up – the view over the roofs is worth it.

Town hall on the market square

The Lübeck town hall is among the oldest in Germany; it arose in the 13th century and was extended over centuries. The dark façade with the wind holes and the Gothic loggia are characteristic. Tours give insight into the audience hall and other rooms (check dates beforehand).

The market square in front is the city’s meeting point: market stalls, cafés and the main house of Niederegger are within sight.

Tip: Take a tour if you have time – the interior is impressive.

European Hansemuseum

The Hanseatic Museum tells the story of the Hanseatic League, the medieval trade alliance to which Lübeck belonged. In a modern exhibition you walk through scenes, merchants’ offices and city models and learn how trade connected cities, countries and cultures.

Plan two to three hours; admission and opening times change (check beforehand). The museum lies next to the Burgkirche at the end of the old town and is a good rainy-day tip.

Tip: Plan it for a rainy day.

Buddenbrook House & literature

The Buddenbrook House in Mengstraße was the family home of the grandparents of Thomas and Heinrich Mann and is now a literature museum. It tells of the brothers, their work and of the city that shaped Thomas Mann’s novel “Buddenbrooks”.

Only a few minutes’ walk away lies the Günter Grass House, dedicated to the Nobel laureate in literature. If you are interested in literature both houses are a highlight. Check times and prices beforehand.

Tip: Combine the Buddenbrook House and Grass House in one walk.

2. Hidden gems

Hidden passages and courtyards💎 Hidden gem

Through the passages and courtyards of the old town – most visitors walk past them.

In the old town there are around 90 preserved passages and courtyards, narrow walkways between the houses that arose from the Middle Ages for the housing of the poorer population. Today many courtyards are restored and lovingly planted – you enter them through low gates on the street and suddenly stand in a quiet oasis.

Especially beautiful are for example the Füchtingshof (17th century) in Glockengießerstraße and the Glandorps Gang. Many courtyards are inhabited: be quiet, photograph respectfully and heed signs.

Tip: Look for low gates and signs on the façades – they are often passageways.

Schiffergesellschaft – the house of 1535💎 Hidden gem

The House of the Shipping Company in Breite Straße is among the oldest inns in the city. The room is furnished with ship models, old benches and wood panelling – you eat where sailors dined centuries ago. It is a popular place for a North German meal, for example labskaus or fish dishes.

A reservation makes sense on weekends; prices are in the upper range. If you only want to look, ask whether you may peek in briefly.

Tip: Reserve at weekends and try a regional dish.

Salt storehouses & the Trave waterfront💎 Hidden gem

Right next to the Holstentor stand the salt storehouses, several brick buildings in which salt from Lüneburg was once stored before being shipped to Scandinavia. From here a path leads along the river to museums, cafés and landing stages.

A walk by the water gives you the best view of the old town’s silhouette with the towers. In summer you can take a boat tour on the waters around the old-town island (check times and prices).

Tip: Go at sunset, then the brick glows.

📍 View in Google Maps

Holy Spirit Hospital💎 Hidden gem

The Holy Spirit Hospital of 1286 is among the oldest social institutions in Europe. The large hall with its wall paintings and the former cubicles of the residents is impressive; in Advent a well-known Christmas market takes place here.

Even outside Advent a look inside is worthwhile: the hall is quiet and shows how people used to live. Check opening times beforehand.

Tip: In Advent you come for the Christmas market, otherwise for the quiet.

Travemünde – Baltic beach💎 Hidden gem

About 20 kilometres north lies Travemünde, Lübeck’s Baltic seaside resort. A beach promenade, sandy beach, spa park and the four-masted barque “Passat” as a museum ship invite you to a day by the sea. You reach it by regional train or bus in about 30 minutes (check timetables beforehand).

In summer the beach is crowded; in spring or autumn you have more space. A fish roll on the promenade is part of it.

Tip: Go in spring or autumn and eat a fish roll on the promenade.

FAQ

How far is Lübeck from Hamburg?

About 45 minutes by regional train.

How many days do you need in Lübeck?
One day is enough for the highlights, with Travemünde and museums it is two.
Is Lübeck a UNESCO World Heritage site?
Yes, the old town has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987 – as a large old-town ensemble one of the first in Germany.
